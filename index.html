I — THE ARTIST

The instrument disappears.
The breath remains.

Chinmay Goud plays the bansuri — the wooden flute of North Indian classical music. Trained in raga and the long tone, he treats performance less as display than as listening: a room, a breath, and the silence between notes.

On a wedding morning or under a club's low light, the sound is the same — warm, unforced, alive. The music does not arrive to impress. It arrives to stay.

V — THE INSTRUMENT

Wood, air,
and patience.

A single length of bamboo, drilled and tuned by hand. No keys, no reeds — only the shape of the breath and the place a fingertip rests. The bansuri is one of the oldest voices in Indian music, and one of the least forgiving.

KEY
A · Bass A
MATERIAL
Assam bamboo
RANGE
Two and a half octaves
HOLES
Seven fingers · one breath
VOICE
Raga · long tone

TRAINING & PATH

VI — LIVE

Where the flute
has travelled.

Four rooms, four different silences. The programme is shaped around the occasion — never the other way around.

01

Weddings

Vidai, pheras, and the first hush of the mandap — the bansuri as a thread of tenderness through the day.

02

Celebrations

House warmings, anniversaries, and evenings that should not end quickly. Music that sits close to the table.

03

Clubs

Amplified and unhurried — classical phrases moving over a low pulse, until the room forgets the hour.

04

Orchestras

Bansuri in concert — raga and orchestra in conversation, the wooden voice against strings.
